## Why sleep and ADHD often clash

Researchers have looked closely at sleep in ADHD. A review of 62 studies found a steady link between ADHD and being a "night owl" [1].

Your body clock is the inner timer that tells you when to feel sleepy. In people with ADHD, it tends to run later [1]. That can mean falling asleep later than you'd like [1].

The alarm for work still goes off at the same time, though. The habits below can help. None of them cost anything.

## 1. Keep one wake-up time

Going to bed and waking up at around the same time each day helps your body set a routine [2]. Health and Wellbeing Queensland says to do this even on weekends [3].

If you can only change one thing, start with your wake-up time. Set it, and keep it.

## 2. Set an alarm to start winding down

A calming bedtime routine tells your body it's time to wind down [3]. Pick two or three easy steps and do them in the same order each night.

- Dim the lights.
- Have a warm shower.
- Read a paper book.

An evening can vanish into "one more episode". So set a phone alarm for the start of your wind-down, not just for waking up. When it goes off, the routine begins.

## 3. Screens and light at night

The blue light from screens can interfere with your body's natural sleep signals [3]. Health and Wellbeing Queensland suggests no TV or phone scrolling for at least an hour before bed [3].

healthdirect suggests keeping screens out of the bedroom altogether [5]. One easy way: charge your phone in another room. Use a basic alarm clock instead.

## 4. Time your caffeine

healthdirect says to avoid tea, coffee and caffeinated soft drinks for at least 3 to 7 hours before bed [2]. Its insomnia advice goes further: keep caffeine to mornings only [5].

It also suggests avoiding alcohol for at least 4 hours before bed [2].

## 5. Keep the bed for sleep

Use your bed for sleep, not screens [2]. Health and Wellbeing Queensland suggests making your bed a place for sleeping only [4]. Keep the room quiet, dark and at a comfortable temperature [2].

Can't sleep? Go to another room and do something quiet, like reading, until you feel tired [5]. Try not to check the time. Clock-watching can make you more anxious about not sleeping [2].

## 6. Park racing thoughts on paper

Does your mind start planning tomorrow the moment your head hits the pillow? Writing your thoughts in a journal can help clear your mind [3].

- Keep a notepad near your bed, not your phone.
- Jot down to-dos and worries, then close it.
- Try to deal with worries earlier in the day, not at bedtime [5].

healthdirect calls this a "buffer zone" before bed. It's a time when you're not solving problems or planning the next day [2].

## 7. Get daylight in the morning

Bright light early in the day is one of Health and Wellbeing Queensland's sleep tips [4]. Step outside for a few minutes soon after you wake up.

Ideas that take no extra time:

- Have breakfast or your morning coffee outside.
- Walk part of the way to work.
- Open the blinds as soon as your alarm goes off.

## When to see a GP about sleep

Habits are a good start, but some sleep problems need a check-up. A GP can look at whether a health issue or a sleep disorder is behind poor sleep [4].

See your GP if:

- you snore loudly, or someone notices you stop breathing while you sleep [6]
- you wake up gasping or choking [6]
- you feel very sleepy during the day [6]
- you often wake up not feeling refreshed, or tiredness is affecting your mood [2]
- tiredness makes driving feel unsafe.

The last one matters. Lack of sleep slows your reactions, which can make driving dangerous [2]. Obstructive sleep apnoea, where breathing pauses during sleep, also raises the risk of car and workplace accidents [6].

If needed, your GP can refer you to a sleep specialist for diagnosis and treatment [6].

## Sleep and your ADHD care

Thinking about an ADHD assessment? Jot down your sleep for a week or two first. Note when you go to bed, when you wake, and when you have caffeine.
