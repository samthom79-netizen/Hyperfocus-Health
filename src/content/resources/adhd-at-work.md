## Your rights at work

In Australia, the Disability Discrimination Act protects workers with disability from unfair treatment. Its meaning of disability is broad. It includes neurological, learning and psychiatric disabilities, even if others can't tell or it doesn't affect you all the time [1].

This page is general information, not legal advice. If you're unsure how it applies to you, get advice for your own situation.

## What "reasonable adjustments" means

Reasonable adjustments are changes at work that help a worker with disability do their job safely and well. They can change how work is organised, the space you work in, or the way tasks are done [1].

The law says employers must consider how they could make reasonable adjustments to help you do the job [1]. They don't have to if it would cause "unjustifiable hardship". That means major difficulty or unreasonable cost, and the employer would need to show how [1].

Many adjustments cost little or nothing [1]. You still need to be able to do the main duties of the job. The law calls these the "inherent requirements" [1].

## Do you have to tell your employer?

Whether to tell people at work is your choice [2]. If your ADHD doesn't affect your work or your safety, you don't have to mention it [2].

You should talk to your employer if it affects how you do your job, or your ability to work safely [2]. Think about this carefully if your job has safety risks for you or others.

If you do tell them, you only need to share what relates to your work or safety. You don't need to share other medical or personal details [2]. Privacy laws mean people at work can't pass it on without your permission [2].

Not sure what to do? JobAccess advisers give free, confidential advice [3].

## Adjustments people with ADHD often ask for

What helps is different for each person [3]. These are common starting points.

**Clearer instructions**

- Written instructions for complex tasks [3]
- Written recaps of spoken conversations [5]
- Clear priorities and deadlines [3]

**Fewer distractions**

- A quieter workspace, or a desk away from other people [3] [4]
- Noise-cancelling headphones if the space is noisy [5]
- One task at a time, where possible [4]

**Structure and reminders**

- Big tasks broken into small steps [4]
- A to-do list or checklist you can tick off [4]
- Calendars, watches and timers with prompts [4]
- Regular check-ins with your manager to review your workload [4]
- Focus-heavy tasks early in the day, with regular breaks [4]

**Flexibility and tools**

- Flexible hours or work location [5]
- Software or other technology that supports your work tasks [3]

## Your right to ask for flexible hours

Workers with disability can also make a formal request for flexible work. Examples include changing your start and finish times, or working from home [6]. You usually need to have worked for the same employer for at least 12 months, and casual workers have extra conditions [6].

The request must be in writing. It must say what you want changed and why [6]. Your employer must reply in writing no later than 21 days after getting it. They can only say no on reasonable business grounds, and only after discussing it with you [6].

## How to ask

A short written request gives you and your employer a clear record. For flexible hours, writing is required [6]. Whether you mention ADHD is up to you [2].

Try this simple structure:

1. **What's hard:** "I find it hard to keep track of spoken instructions."
2. **What would help:** "Could you send key tasks by email after meetings?"
3. **Why it helps the work:** "This will help me meet my deadlines."
4. **A trial:** "Could we try it for a month, then check in?"

Keep a copy of what you send and any reply.

## Help from JobAccess and the EAF

JobAccess can help you work out which changes might suit your job. Call 1800 464 800 for free, confidential advice [3].

The Australian Government's Employment Assistance Fund (EAF) can help with the cost of reasonable adjustments [1]. It can cover:

- workplace changes and equipment, including devices to control noise [7]
- assistive technology and software [7]
- specialised support and training for people with learning disabilities or mental health conditions [7]
- awareness training for your workplace [7]

For people with ADHD, this can include one-to-one support from a qualified professional to build strategies for work [3].

The EAF won't pay for things an employer would normally provide, like a desk or computer [7]. JobAccess looks at each application on its own, and you'll be asked for medical evidence [3]. Costs are paid back after you apply and get approval [7].

## If things go wrong

Start by raising it with your manager or a contact person at work [1]. If that doesn't fix it, you can complain to the Australian Human Rights Commission for free on 1300 656 419 [1].

The Fair Work Commission can help with disputes about flexible work requests [6]. Legal advice or your union may also help [1].
