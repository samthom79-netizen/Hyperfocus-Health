## What an assessment is for

An ADHD assessment is a careful look at your whole life, not a single test. A trained clinician (the doctor or health professional doing the assessment) works out whether ADHD explains what you're living with [1]. A questionnaire or rating scale can't make the diagnosis on its own [2].

## What the clinician looks at

It involves interviews, plus medical and psychological assessments [3]. It covers three big areas.

- **Your symptoms now.** Trouble keeping attention, restlessness, or acting on impulse. For a diagnosis, symptoms need to have lasted more than six months [3].
- **Your history back to childhood.** ADHD starts before age 12, so the clinician looks for signs it began early [1]. For adults, this has to be established as part of the diagnosis [3].
- **More than one area of life.** Symptoms need to show up in two or more settings, such as home, work, study or social life [1][2]. They also need to cause real problems, not just the odd lost key [2].

Expect questions about your development, mental health, physical health, family and work [2]. The clinician also notices how you are on the day, which doctors call a "mental state assessment" [2]. Questionnaires may be used as an extra tool [1][2].

## Why someone who knew you growing up helps

Guidelines recommend information from other people, not just from you [2]. International guidelines also treat information from several people as a key part of the process [1].

A parent, older sibling or long-term partner can help. Your doctor might speak with them to understand how your symptoms affect your daily life [3]. If there's no one who can help, say so early, and bring any school reports you have [3].

## Other conditions that are checked for

Plenty of things can affect focus, energy and mood. So the clinician checks for conditions that could be the real cause, or that sit alongside ADHD [1].

Things that can look like ADHD include:

- sleep problems, such as obstructive sleep apnoea (breathing stops and starts during sleep) or not getting enough sleep [1]
- other mental health or developmental conditions with overlapping signs [1]

Conditions that often occur alongside ADHD include [1][3]:

- anxiety and depression
- bipolar disorder
- personality disorders
- drug or alcohol problems (called "substance use disorders")
- learning difficulties and autism

Having one of these doesn't rule ADHD out. The Australian guideline says no particular condition must be excluded first [1]. But it can change what care suits you, and who should provide it [4].

## Physical health checks

Your physical health is part of the picture [1][2]. Before any treatment starts, guidelines recommend:

- a medical history, including heart problems in you or close family [2]
- height and weight [1][2]
- pulse and blood pressure [2]
- a check of your heart health [1][2]

A heart tracing (ECG) isn't needed for everyone. It may be suggested if your own or your family's history raises a concern [2].

## Possible outcomes

An assessment can end in a few different ways.

- **A diagnosis of ADHD.** The clinician explains what they found and talks you through a treatment plan. Queensland Government advice supports a holistic approach, meaning care that covers daily life, not just symptoms [4].
- **No diagnosis of ADHD.** Having symptoms of ADHD doesn't always mean you have it [4]. The clinician should still explain what they think is going on and what could help next.
- **A referral.** A specialist GP may need to refer you to a psychiatrist (a doctor who specialises in mental health). This is more likely if you have other health conditions [4].

## How to prepare

You don't need all of these. Bring what you can.

- **School reports.** You may be asked for them [3]. Teacher comments can show what you were like in class.
- **Earlier assessments or letters.** For example, from learning support, counselling or past testing.
- **Notes about daily life.** Jot down real examples, like missed deadlines or unfinished jobs. Note where they happen (work, home, friends), since the assessment looks at more than one area [1][2].
- **Your health details.** Health conditions you have, anything you take regularly, and any heart problems in close family [2].
- **A person who knew you growing up.** Ask early if they're happy to help.

Notes on your phone are fine. Short dot points are plenty.
